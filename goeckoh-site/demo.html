Corollary Discharge Mechanism

The 200 ms Prediction Window

Every time your motor cortex issues a speech command, it simultaneously sends an efference copy — a prediction of what the resulting sound should sound like — to your auditory cortex. The auditory cortex has roughly 200 milliseconds to compare what it actually hears against that prediction. If the heard sound arrives within this window and matches the target, the motor program is reinforced. If the prediction fails or the signal arrives late, the learning loop does not close.

In ASD and dysarthria, the child produces a distorted vowel, the brain predicts a distorted vowel, the sounds match — and the broken pattern is reinforced every time. Goeckoh breaks this cycle by delivering a corrected signal within the window.

0 ms
Motor command issued — you intend to speak
<50 ms
Goeckoh delivers corrected signal to earbuds*
~200 ms
Prediction window closes — brain finalises motor learning
250 ms
Learning window closed — no motor update possible
* <50 ms refers to the native on-device processing latency of Goeckoh's DSP engine (Rust-compiled, running directly on the device's audio hardware). This demo shows analysis only; the correction is delivered through the full application running natively. Total perceived latency also includes Bluetooth earbud round-trip, which varies by hardware (typically 40–150 ms). Even at the upper range, correction arrives well within the 200 ms prediction window.

N1 Suppression: When the correction arrives on time, the auditory cortex suppresses the N1 evoked potential — an EEG marker indicating the brain accepted the corrected voice as self-produced. This is the measurable neural signature of the corollary discharge loop closing correctly. (Niziolek, Nagarajan, Houde, Journal of Neuroscience, 2013.)
Acoustic science — what each metric means
🎵
F0 — Fundamental Frequency (Pitch)
— Hz
The rate at which your vocal folds open and snap shut. Every second, an adult male's folds close ~116 times; an adult female's ~205 times; a child's ~223 times. That repetition rate is what you hear as pitch.

Goeckoh measures F0 by autocorrelation in the 80–500 Hz range and preserves it exactly — the correction never moves pitch. Your speaker identity lives in F0.
Adult male: 93–135 Hz · Adult female: 162–238 Hz · Child: ~223 Hz
↕️
F1 — First Formant (Tongue Height)
— Hz
The lowest resonance peak of your vocal tract. F1 is controlled by jaw openness and tongue height. Open your jaw wide — F1 rises. Raise your tongue toward the palate — F1 falls.

In ASD and dysarthria, F1 often fails to reach its target because jaw excursion is reduced (articulatory undershoot). This is one of the primary formant targets Goeckoh corrects.
Typical range across vowels: 300–900 Hz
↔️
F2 — Second Formant (Tongue Advancement)
— Hz
The second resonance peak. F2 encodes tongue front/back position. Push your tongue forward — F2 rises (front vowels like /i/). Pull it back — F2 falls (back vowels like /u/).

In dysarthria, back vowels are the hardest to produce correctly — both lip-rounding and tongue retraction are reduced simultaneously, pushing F2 into front-vowel territory. This is measurable and correctable.
Typical range across vowels: 800–2500 Hz
〰️
HNR — Harmonics-to-Noise Ratio
— dB
The ratio of periodic (harmonic) energy to turbulent noise in your glottal signal. A high HNR means your vocal folds are vibrating cleanly and regularly. A low HNR indicates breathy, rough, or pathological voice quality.

HNR is computed from the autocorrelation peak at the F0 lag period. Goeckoh logs HNR continuously during enrollment to characterise your baseline vocal health.
Healthy adult on sustained vowel: ≥11 dB · Pathological threshold: ≤7.4 dB
🌊
What is a Formant?
Vocal tract resonance
Your throat, mouth, and nasal cavity form a ~17 cm resonant tube. Like any tube, it has natural frequencies where sound energy is amplified — these are the formants.

When vocal fold buzz travels up through the tract, certain frequencies get boosted (at formants) and others get suppressed. The resulting spectral envelope defines the vowel. Different mouth shapes = different formant positions = different vowels.

This is Fant's source-filter theory (1960): the folds produce the source; the tract is the filter. They are independent. Goeckoh corrects only the filter — never the source.
F1 encodes height · F2 encodes front/back · F3 encodes rhotics + identity
